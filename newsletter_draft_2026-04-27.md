# The Fight Docket — April 27, 2026

**Issue Title: The Fight Game on Two Fronts: Dana White Under Oath and the Battle for Boxing's Soul**

---

## INTRO / EDITOR'S NOTE

There are weeks in this sport when the noise from inside the arenas almost drowns out the noise from inside the courtrooms. This wasn't one of those weeks. This was a week when the courtrooms were loud enough to shake the rafters, and when the arenas delivered a moment so frightening it put everything else into perspective.

Start with Shadasia Green. On April 17, at the Infosys Theater inside Madison Square Garden, Green walked out to defend her IBF and WBO super middleweight titles against a challenger nobody was talking about as a major threat. She left on a stretcher. Three days later, she posted from the ICU that she had blood on her brain. No surgery needed, she said, but the words "blood on my brain" hit this sport like they always do, like a reminder that nothing else we argue about on any given week really matters in the same way.

Green is recovering. That's the most important sentence in this issue.

And then, the following Saturday in Las Vegas, a UFC Fight Night that tied an all-time record for decisions on a single card reminded everyone how unfinished the conversation about fighter compensation really is. Eleven decisions. One card. In that same week, on Capitol Hill, senators grilled boxing's biggest figures about the Muhammad Ali Boxing Revival Act, the legislation that has been making its way through Congress for years and now feels more real than ever. Oscar De La Hoya left the hearing frustrated. Nico Ali Walsh left conflicted. The fight game is genuinely at an inflection point, and whether the people inside it want to acknowledge that or not, this week made it impossible to look away.

We've got two monster fight weekends coming, Dana White's missing phones at the center of a federal courthouse in Nevada, and more. Let's get into it.

## MAIN STORY

Dana White took a seat in federal court in Las Vegas on February 4 and 5, and what emerged from that spoliation hearing in Johnson v. Zuffa was one of the most remarkable scenes in the history of combat sports litigation. I'm told by sources following the case closely that the two-day evidentiary hearing in the U.S. District Court for the District of Nevada produced testimony that should concern anyone paying attention to how the UFC does business.

Here's what happened. Plaintiffs in the case, fighters who competed from July 1, 2017 forward, alleged that TKO Operating Co., Endeavor Group Holdings, and Zuffa LLC destroyed years of critical evidence and covered up what they did. Their lawyers moved for severe sanctions, including default judgment. The hearing Judge Richard F. Boulware II presided over was supposed to determine whether that happened.

White took the stand and, under oath, admitted to missing years of text messages across various phones. He said he wasn't "tech-savvy." He acknowledged losing a phone within the last year. He testified that he no longer handles fighter negotiations, that he handed that role to Hunter Campbell around 2016. And in one exchange that lawyers for the plaintiffs will likely lean on heavily, White acknowledged saying to Campbell, "Congrats, you get to do this."

Tracy Long, UFC's Vice President of Athlete Compliance and Regulatory, didn't help the organization's case either. Long testified that she prepares contracts based on instructions from matchmakers Hunter Campbell and Sean Shelby. She gave frequent "I don't know" and "I don't recall" responses. She acknowledged using screenshots of text messages to document deals. And there were flagged missing communications involving the Ilia Topuria contract hold-up before his fight with Charles Oliveira at UFC 317 last June.

Judge Boulware was not subtle. He noted that missing phones and records represented "sanctionable offenses" and ordered the UFC to make a full accounting of the missing devices, with a resolution target of end of April 2026. That deadline, if you are reading this on Monday morning, is this week.

The backdrop here matters. This is the follow-on case to Le v. Zuffa, which settled for $375 million in February 2025, covering more than 1,100 fighters from December 2010 through June 2017. Judge Boulware himself, in a ruling during that case, found that Zuffa had acted anticompetitively and written of fighters being "trapped by Zuffa's exclusionary contracts," with the UFC holding "monopsony power" over fighter services. The American Antitrust Institute named Le v. Zuffa the winner of its 2025 Antitrust Enforcement Award.

Now there are two additional cases in the District of Nevada: Cirkunovs v. Zuffa (Case No. 2:25-cv-00914), filed in May 2025, challenging arbitration and class action waivers added to fighter contracts after the Le settlement, and Davis v. Zuffa (Case No. 2:25-cv-00946), also filed May 2025, which takes the fight to fighters who competed for promotions other than UFC, arguing the organization's suppression of competition affected everyone in the sport.

The UFC's response to all of this has largely been to invoke those same arbitration clauses. But the spoliation issue is different. Document destruction, if proven with intent, can flip a case entirely. Judge Boulware knows exactly what these cases are about. And Dana White, under oath, was not a convincing witness for a company still fighting billion-dollar antitrust exposure.

My take: the end of April accounting deadline Judge Boulware set is a pivot point in this litigation. Watch it closely.

## FIGHT CARD PREVIEWS

**Benavidez vs. Ramirez, May 2, Las Vegas (PPV/DAZN/Amazon Prime)**

The super middleweight division has been one of boxing's most loaded weight classes for the better part of five years, and this Saturday night it gets a clarity fight. David Benavidez, the undefeated American Monster from Phoenix, steps in against Gilberto "Zurdo" Ramirez in what is essentially a de facto number one contender fight at 168 pounds with a path to the undisputed crown in clear view.

Benavidez is 29-0 and one of the most physically imposing fighters in boxing, a volume puncher who walks through adversity and throws leather until something lands clean. Ramirez has good size, a hard left hand, and wins the rounds he controls early. The question is always whether he can maintain his composure when a big puncher starts tying up his work. I've seen Ramirez hesitate when things get physical late in fights, and Benavidez does not hesitate. I like Benavidez by TKO somewhere in the second half. The division is waiting.

**Naoya Inoue vs. Junto Nakatani, May 2, Tokyo Dome (DAZN)**

Same night, different world. On the other side of the planet, the best pound-for-pound fighter alive defends his undisputed super bantamweight titles in the most iconic boxing venue in Japan. Naoya Inoue against Junto Nakatani is a stylistically fascinating fight because Nakatani is genuinely skilled, a former flyweight champion who has grown into his 122-pound frame and carries real power. But Inoue is Inoue. He is simply operating at a level that most fighters in this era, at any weight, cannot touch. The Tokyo Dome atmosphere for this fight will be incredible. Set your alarm if you care about watching a true all-time great.

**Wardley vs. Dubois, May 9, Manchester (DAZN)**

British heavyweight supremacy is back on the table. Fabio Wardley and Daniel Dubois have both arrived at the point in their careers where the winner of this fight has a legitimate claim to be the top British heavyweight, and in the current heavyweight landscape, that is meaningful. Dubois had a breakthrough run against Usyk that nobody expected. Wardley is as physically powerful as anyone at 200-plus pounds in England. This is a collision of styles and punching power, and I would not be shocked if it ends dramatically in either direction.

## BUSINESS INTEL

The biggest business story in boxing right now is not a deal that has been signed. It's a deal that is almost signed. Eddie Hearn confirmed this week that Tyson Fury versus Anthony Joshua is in the final stages of negotiation. "We will have a contract," Hearn said, "and we haven't signed a contract yet, but we are negotiating the final points in an agreement to come back to the ring." That is about as close as Hearn gets to a guarantee before ink is dry. Fury and Joshua have been circling each other for years, blocked by contracts, injuries, and a heavyweight division that kept reshuffling before they could get there. If this actually gets signed, and I am told by people familiar with the discussions that both sides want it done before summer, it would be the biggest British boxing event in a generation and, depending on the venue, one of the biggest events globally in years.

Most Valuable Promotions, the operation backed by Jake Paul and his team, held its first U.S. card on April 17 at the Infosys Theater inside Madison Square Garden, and there is a new ESPN broadcast deal attached to the venture. The fact that MVP's lead fighter on that card was Lani Daniels, a New Zealand women's super middleweight who was almost entirely unknown to American audiences, tells you something about the strategy: build the platform, let the fights do the talking, grow from there. Daniels won the IBF and WBO titles in a performance that nobody expected. The ESPN deal gives MVP distribution that matters. Paul's ability to leverage media attention has been consistent, even when the fights themselves have been uneven.

Richardson Hitchins vacated the IBF super lightweight title this week and is moving up to welterweight, where he reportedly has eyes on Ryan Garcia and Devin Haney. His decision opened up the IBF 140-pound title, and Arthur Biyarslanov, a 21-0 Russian-born Canadian who last fought April 9 in Montreal, is now in position for that fight. Teofimo Lopez had been offered the IBF mandate at 140 before Hitchins vacated, and Lopez passed on it to focus on welterweight. The dominoes are moving.

The Lawrence Okolie versus Tony Yoka fight scheduled for Britain was canceled this week after a drug test failure. Details on which fighter failed are still emerging, but the cancellation is a significant blow to the UK heavyweight scene, which had already lost some momentum.

## LEGAL DOCKET

There are three active federal cases in the District of Nevada with TKO and Zuffa as defendants, and this week underscored just how consequential they are.

In Johnson v. Zuffa LLC (Case No. 2:21-cv-01189), the spoliation hearing from February 4-5 produced Dana White's sworn testimony about missing phones and lost text messages, and Judge Richard Boulware set an end-of-April 2026 deadline for the UFC to account for all missing devices. That deadline falls this week. The plaintiffs moved for default judgment as a sanction, arguing the organization destroyed years of critical evidence. Separately, plaintiff's counsel has sought a contempt of court ruling against Dominance MMA, the management company run by Ali Abdelaziz, for discovery violations in the same case. Class coverage for Johnson runs from July 1, 2017 to present.

Cirkunovs v. Zuffa (Case No. 2:25-cv-00914) challenges the validity of arbitration and class action waivers that the UFC added to fighter contracts after the Le v. Zuffa settlement. Filed May 2025, the case is ongoing and represents what many fighter advocates see as the UFC's attempt to firewall itself from future class action exposure. Davis v. Zuffa (Case No. 2:25-cv-00946), also filed May 2025, expands the theory further: it argues that fighters who competed for other promotions were harmed by the UFC's monopolistic practices because the suppression of competition affected pay across all of professional MMA.

On Capitol Hill, the Muhammad Ali Boxing Revival Act cleared the House and went to a Senate Commerce Committee hearing on April 22. It did not go smoothly. Oscar De La Hoya, who testified in favor of the legislation, said the hearing "felt like an uphill battle" and left frustrated. Nico Ali Walsh, grandson of the man for whom the act is named, told reporters that "the cons outweigh the pros" after the session. Eddie Hearn's position: "The decision's already made." What that means in practice is unclear, but the bill's path through the Senate is not guaranteed.

## FIGHTER WATCH

**Aljamain Sterling** is not going away quietly at featherweight. Sterling beat Youssef Zalal on Saturday by unanimous decision, 49-45 on all three cards, and immediately called out Movsar Evloev, the only man to beat him since he moved up in weight, along with champion Alexander Volkanovski. He is now 18-5 in the UFC, 26-5 overall, and he said after Saturday's win that he is coming with "no pause." The knock on Sterling at featherweight has always been that he is undersized for the upper tier of the division. He doesn't seem to care. He has been one of the most underrated competitive stories in MMA for the last few years, grinding out wins in a weight class where he is rarely given a physical advantage. The decision-heavy card around him did him no favors in terms of a highlight moment, but his numbers are accumulating.

**Lani Daniels** is a name you should know. The 37-year-old from Pipiwai, New Zealand came to New York on April 17 as the underdog challenger and left with two world title belts and a story that boxing will be telling for a while. Daniels entered 12-4-2, won titles at light heavyweight and heavyweight in New Zealand previously, and now holds the IBF and WBO belts at super middleweight in what was her biggest fight and first on American soil. That she turned in a performance dominant enough to stop a 16-2 former unified champion in the ninth round, inside Madison Square Garden, in the first American card of the promotion backing her, is genuinely extraordinary. She is 37. Most fighters at that age are winding down. Daniels is just getting to the main stage.

**Jarrell Miller** is the WBA heavyweight mandatory challenger now. "Big Baby" Miller won a unanimous decision over Lenier Pero at the Fontainebleau Las Vegas on Saturday, 117-111 on two cards and 115-113 on the third, working down the WBA's No. 2-ranked opponent over twelve rounds. Miller is 28-1-2, 37 years old, and his path back to relevance after years of failed drug tests and professional turbulence has been one of boxing's more improbable second acts. He called out Deontay Wilder after Saturday. Fights with Anthony Joshua and Oleksandr Usyk are being discussed in his camp. The mandatory position makes him impossible to ignore.

## THE TAKE

Here's the thing about the Ali Act Senate hearing: the people who testified in favor of it were not particularly convincing, and the people who are supposed to benefit from it are deeply divided, and none of that actually means the legislation is wrong.

De La Hoya feeling like he was fighting uphill at a Senate Commerce Committee hearing is not a surprise. He walked in as a former promoter with mixed baggage, and senators were never going to treat him like a reformer. Nico Ali Walsh saying "the cons outweigh the pros" was more revealing, because it surfaced something that has always been complicated about the Ali Act push: the fighters and former fighters who understand the sport best are not uniformly behind it.

But here's what I keep coming back to. The core argument for reform in boxing, the argument that fighters deserve better financial transparency, contract protections, and a structure that doesn't allow a single promoter to effectively own a champion for life, is correct. It has always been correct. The sport has been exploiting fighters in the open for decades, and the fact that boxing's own stakeholders can't agree on how to fix it does not make the exploitation less real. It just makes the fix harder.

The UFC's antitrust cases running through the District of Nevada are, in many ways, the MMA version of the same argument. Judge Boulware already found monopsony. The fighters were trapped. The promoter held "unfettered power." The settlement cost $375 million and counting.

Both sports are having the same underlying argument right now, about who the sport belongs to. Not the promoters, not the networks, not the governing bodies. The fighters. That argument is not going away. And despite the frustration coming out of that Senate hearing, I think we are closer to meaningful change in boxing than we have been in a long time.

The hearing was messy. The path forward is unclear. But the bill is moving, and the moment in American boxing law is real.
